### Collective Principles & Documentation

#### 1. Introduction

Across our projects, we have explored how sports culture becomes transformed into data through digital media platforms, news coverage, and computational methods. While our datasets focused on different sports and media environments, we collectively encountered similar questions surrounding visibility, interpretation, scale, and representation. 

#### 2. Core Principles for Working with Sports as Data

- Principle 1: Cultural data is not naturally found (categorization and interpretation are decided from humans) 
None of our datasets existed in a ready-made form. Before collecting data, we had to decide how to define and organize it. Questions like what counts as a “controversy,” whether coverage is “negative” or “mixed,” or if follower counts reflect athletic success all required interpretation. These choices directly influenced the conclusions our projects could make. Because of this, future researchers should clearly explain how categories are defined and recognize that these decisions are subjective rather than completely neutral.

- Principle 2: Visibility is not evenly distributed
Each project used computational methods like web scraping, APIs, sentiment analysis, or LLM classification to collect data at a larger scale. However, increasing scale also changed what became visible in the dataset. In Maya’s project, expanding from manually labeled NFL events to hundreds of LLM-labeled examples introduced classification issues and reduced nuance. In the Kevin Durant project, automated scraping improved verification compared to manually gathered links. Scaling up data collection does not simply produce more data — it can fundamentally change the type and quality of information being captured.

- Principle 3: Computation scales data, but also reshapes meaning/interpretation
Across all projects, the data we collected was heavily influenced by outside systems such as platform algorithms, media attention, and search engine behavior. Volleyball players from countries with stronger media infrastructures often had much larger followings than equally talented players elsewhere. NFL controversy coverage focused more heavily on already high-profile athletes. Similarly, Google News surfaced more articles from Kevin Durant’s Warriors and Nets years than his earlier Thunder career. These examples show that digital datasets are shaped by existing systems of visibility and popularity, not just by the subjects themselves.

- Principle 4: Human interpretation remains necessary in cultural datasets
Even though automation played a major role, none of our projects were fully automated. Human judgment remained necessary throughout the process. Tiffany manually checked Instagram accounts because automated scraping returned incorrect profiles. Colin reviewed YouTube results to confirm relevance to specific matches. Maya compared LLM classifications with her own interpretations to identify disagreements. Adam adjusted his methodology when API limitations made large-scale LLM labeling unrealistic. Decisions about what to verify, trust, or correct are important methodological choices and should be documented alongside the technical workflow.

#### 3. Individual Contributions

Adam: Examined how sports media constructs narratives around athletes over time, using Kevin Durant as a case study. Duran'ts career is stamped by a controversial departure of Oklahoma City, years of public criticism, and a shifting legacy debate. The project involved building a dataset of nearly 500 news artciles, drawn from well-known outlets like ESPN and categorizing each piece by narrative theme and emotional tone. What emerged was not a record of the past, but a record of how sports journalism chose to tell it. In the end I learned that the dataset built from news coverage is never just about the subject itself. It is a reflection of media the moment that decided what was worth covering at the time. 

Tiffany: Examined how social media visibility in professional volleyball is shaped by cultural, institutional, and national factors rather than athletic performance alone. The project involved building a dataset of 100 professional volleyball players from multiple countries and leagues, including variables such as nationality, Olympian status, national team membership, championships won, Instagram following counts, and Instagram post counts. What initially began as a project about whether better players gain more followers evolved into a broader investigation of how digital visibility is socially constructed through media systems, fan culture, globalization, and national sports infrastructure. Throughout the process, I learned that social media metrics do not function as neutral indicators of popularity or athletic skill. Instead, they reflect larger systems of cultural visibility, institutional prestige, and platform dynamics. The project also demonstrated that working with social media data requires constant negotiation between computational efficiency and human interpretation, especially when verifying identities, distinguishing official accounts from fan pages, and interpreting popularity across different cultural contexts.

Colin: Examined how professional men's tennis matches were received by fans on social media. Through YouTube highlight videos and fan comments, the project combined structured tennis match data with video metadata and scrapped comments, likes, and sentiment scores. The project, focused on five major players, began focused on match outcomes, rankings, and upsets. Itn the end it turned into a project based on how fans turn a match performance into a narrative, usually involving rivalry, personality, and sometimes legacy. The final dataset showed that tennis culture is shaped by not only who wins/loses, but also by which matches are made visible thorugh official channels and search results. This project showed that computational tools can help scale cultural analysis, but it also flattens some of the meaning. The sentiment analyzer struggled with some sarcasm, context, or sports related topics. Human judgment still remained necessary when selecting the kinds of matches, interpreting some comments, and ultimately deciding what counts as a meaningful fan interaction to sports media. 

Maya: Examined how news outlets covering NFL players influence fans and outsiders' perspectives. This project involved looking at a wide range of news outlets that discussed and informed readers of the event that involved an NFL player. These events can be described as a controversy, apology, etc., which could be described by how the outlets themselves describe those events out in the public. Through the process of upscaling the dataset I had I recognized how LLM differ from my interpretation of different categorical features. As mentioned in my final essay, I discussed how LLM focus on the linguistic aspects more than the contextual ones surrounding the events themselves. This demonstrated how computational scaling can obscure certain cultural naunces, social understandings, and interpretative layers that humans can naturally incorporate when analyzing media and public discourse.
