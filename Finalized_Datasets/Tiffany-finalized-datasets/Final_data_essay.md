#### Visibility Beyond Performance: Social Media, Sports Culture, and Professional Volleyball ####

Professional sports increasingly exist not only within arenas and competitions, but also across digital platforms where athletes are constantly visible, branded, and circulated through social media. Platforms such as Instagram have transformed athletes into public digital figures whose popularity is shaped not only by athletic performance, but also by online engagement, national fan cultures, media visibility, and institutional prestige. This project began with a relatively simple question: is there a relationship between a volleyball player’s athletic success and their social media popularity? However, as I constructed and expanded my dataset over the course of the semester, the project evolved into a broader cultural investigation about how visibility itself is produced in professional sports.

My final dataset contains 80 professional volleyball players from multiple countries, genders, and professional leagues. The dataset includes variables such as nationality, league country, gender, team name, position, years active, Olympian status, national team membership, championships won, award count, Instagram following count, and Instagram post count. Although the project originally focused on correlating performance with follower counts, the data collection process gradually revealed that social media visibility in professional volleyball appears to be shaped more strongly by cultural, institutional, and national systems than by performance alone. This shift in perspective became the most important intellectual development of the project.

The process of creating the dataset was far more complex than I originally expected. Unlike many large datasets that already exist publicly, I built this dataset manually from scratch. I first created a list of professional volleyball players by examining Olympic rosters, national teams, and professional league rosters. I intentionally selected athletes from multiple countries and leagues in order to create a globally representative sample rather than focusing on one nation or one volleyball system. Geographic diversity became especially important because volleyball occupies very different cultural positions depending on the country. In some nations, volleyball is a major spectator sport with substantial media attention and fan engagement, while in others it remains relatively niche despite high levels of athletic competition.

Once the player list was created, I manually verified each athlete’s Instagram account before collecting any social media statistics. This became one of the most time-consuming and methodologically important parts of the project. Many athletes had multiple accounts, fan pages, inactive profiles, or unofficial repost accounts that could easily be mistaken for official accounts. Verification therefore required checking for verification badges, official team links, posting consistency, and cross references with national federations or professional clubs. Initially, I underestimated how difficult identity verification would become within digital environments. I assumed social media data would be relatively straightforward to collect, but I quickly realized that platform ambiguity and identity instability are major challenges in digital cultural research.

This realization significantly changed how I thought about data itself. At the beginning of the semester, I tended to think of data as objective information that could simply be collected and analyzed. However, constructing this dataset demonstrated that data is shaped by human decisions, platform structures, and methodological limitations at every stage. Even something as seemingly simple as determining a player’s “official account” required interpretation and judgment. This process reinforced the idea that datasets are not neutral collections of facts, but constructed representations shaped by choices about inclusion, verification, categorization, and interpretation.

Computation played an important role throughout the project, although not always in the way I originally expected. At first, I assumed that most of the process could be automated through scraping tools and Python scripts. I experimented with Python libraries such as BeautifulSoup, Requests, and Instaloader in order to automate the collection of Instagram statistics. I also explored third-party Instagram statistics websites as potential scraping sources. However, these approaches frequently failed because Instagram aggressively limits scraping, blocks requests, and does not reliably distinguish official accounts from fan pages. Automated systems often returned incorrect profiles, missing data, or inaccessible pages. As a result, fully automated scraping proved unreliable for a project that required accurate identity verification.

Rather than abandoning computation entirely, I adopted a hybrid methodology that combined automation with manual oversight. Computational tools assisted with organization, cleaning, formatting, and portions of data retrieval, while manual verification ensured dataset accuracy. Python scripts helped standardize numerical formatting, organize CSV files, and structure the dataset for analysis. This hybrid approach ultimately became one of the most important lessons of the project: computation is powerful for scaling and organizing data, but cultural datasets still require human interpretation when identity, meaning, and context are involved.

Scale also changed the nature of the project over time. The dataset initially began with approximately 75 athletes and only a few social media variables. At that scale, the project felt manageable and relatively simple. However, as I expanded the dataset and added more institutional and cultural variables, the project became much more analytically complex. Larger scale revealed patterns that were not immediately visible in smaller samples. For example, nationality appeared to have a major influence on follower counts. Players from Japan, Brazil, Turkey, Italy, and Poland frequently maintained substantially larger audiences than players from countries where volleyball has less media visibility, even when the athletes competed at similarly elite levels.

This observation shifted the entire conceptual direction of the project. Originally, I thought follower counts might simply reflect athletic skill or performance. However, the expanded dataset suggested that digital visibility emerges from broader cultural systems. Athletes are not only individual competitors; they are also symbolic representatives of nations, media systems, sponsorship structures, and fan cultures. Japanese volleyball players, for instance, often accumulated extremely large followings due to the strong domestic popularity of volleyball and highly active fan communities. Brazilian and Turkish players similarly benefited from strong national volleyball cultures and media infrastructures. Meanwhile, equally talented athletes from countries where volleyball receives less attention often had dramatically smaller audiences.

These patterns suggested that social media popularity functions less as a direct measure of athletic performance and more as a reflection of institutional visibility and cultural circulation. Posting frequency also failed to explain popularity in straightforward ways. Some athletes posted frequently but maintained relatively modest audiences, while others posted very rarely yet still attracted enormous followings due to Olympic participation, national prestige, or broader media recognition. This challenged my original assumption that more online activity would necessarily generate larger audiences.

The dataset therefore reveals several important aspects of contemporary sports culture. First, it highlights how digital platforms transform athletes into branded public figures whose visibility is shaped by much more than performance statistics alone. Second, it demonstrates how globalization influences sports media, allowing athletes from certain nations or leagues to gain disproportionate international visibility. Third, it shows how online popularity is deeply connected to institutional systems such as Olympic participation, national representation, sponsorship structures, and media ecosystems.

At the same time, the dataset also conceals many important aspects of athlete identity and experience. Social media metrics cannot fully capture fan relationships, emotional engagement, cultural meaning, or the qualitative content of athlete interactions online. Follower counts provide numerical visibility, but they do not reveal how audiences actually interpret or engage with athletes. Additionally, the dataset cannot account for algorithmic amplification, sponsorship arrangements, language barriers, or platform-specific recommendation systems that may influence visibility. In this sense, the dataset both reveals and obscures culture simultaneously. It quantifies certain forms of visibility while leaving many cultural experiences unmeasured.

Ethical and privacy considerations also became important throughout the project. Although all data was collected from publicly accessible accounts, I became increasingly aware that public availability does not automatically eliminate ethical concerns. Social media data exists within contexts of surveillance, branding, and platform capitalism. Athletes may publicly display aspects of their lives online, but transforming that information into structured datasets still raises questions about consent, interpretation, and representation. I also needed to ensure that the project did not unintentionally misrepresent athletes through inaccurate account identification or misleading comparisons between follower counts and athletic ability.

Another ethical concern involved avoiding simplistic interpretations of popularity. A major lesson of the project was recognizing that social media visibility is not an objective measure of personal value or athletic talent. Treating follower counts as neutral indicators of success risks reinforcing existing inequalities within sports media systems. Athletes from countries with less commercial visibility may receive far less online attention despite competing at the same professional level as globally recognized stars. Because of this, the dataset must be interpreted critically rather than treated as a definitive ranking system.

Throughout the semester, I also learned that cultural data is fundamentally messy. Unlike highly structured scientific datasets, cultural datasets contain ambiguity, instability, and interpretation at nearly every stage. Usernames change, accounts disappear, statistics fluctuate daily, and identity itself becomes difficult to standardize across platforms and countries. Initially, I viewed these inconsistencies as problems to eliminate. Over time, however, I realized that these instabilities are themselves part of the cultural systems being studied. The difficulty of verifying identity online reflects larger issues surrounding digital visibility and platform infrastructure.

This project also connects to broader peer-reviewed scholarship surrounding sports media, digital identity, and platform culture. Scholars such as David Rowe have written extensively about globalization and sports media, particularly how digital technologies reshape the relationship between athletes, audiences, and national identity. Similarly, Alice Marwick’s work on online self-branding and microcelebrity helps explain how athletes increasingly function as public digital brands whose visibility depends on continuous audience engagement and platform performance. Research on platformed identities and influencer culture also provides a useful framework for understanding how athletes navigate visibility within algorithmic media systems.

Additionally, scholarship on digital labor and platform capitalism helps contextualize why social media activity matters economically for athletes today. Athletes are no longer only competitors within sports organizations; they are also content producers operating within attention economies where visibility itself becomes monetizable through sponsorships, endorsements, and branding opportunities. My dataset reflects this broader transformation by showing how athletic visibility increasingly operates across both sports institutions and digital platforms simultaneously.

Ultimately, the most important intellectual shift of this project was moving away from viewing social media metrics as simple indicators of popularity or performance. Instead, I came to understand digital visibility as something culturally produced through overlapping systems of media infrastructure, nationalism, globalization, institutional prestige, branding, and fan culture. The dataset does not simply measure followers; it captures traces of how contemporary sports culture functions within digital media environments.

What began as a relatively straightforward social media dataset ultimately became an exploration of how athletes become visible cultural figures online. The project demonstrated both the power and limitations of computational approaches to culture. Computation made scaling and organizing the dataset possible, but interpretation, verification, and contextual understanding remained deeply human processes. Most importantly, the project revealed that data is never simply “found.” It is constructed through methodological decisions, technological limitations, and cultural assumptions that shape what becomes visible and what remains hidden.
