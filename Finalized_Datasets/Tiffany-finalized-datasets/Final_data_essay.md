#### Visibility Beyond Performance: Social Media, Sports Culture, and Professional Volleyball ####

Professional sports today do not only exist inside stadiums or during games, but also across digital platforms where athletes are constantly visible through social media. Platforms like Instagram have changed athletes into public online figures whose popularity is shaped not only by athletic performance, but also by online engagement, national fan cultures, media attention, and institutional prestige. This project originally started with a simple question: is there a relationship between a volleyball player’s athletic success and their social media popularity? However, as I continued building and expanding my dataset throughout the semester, the project slowly evolved into a broader investigation about how visibility itself is created within professional sports culture.

My final dataset contains over 100 professional volleyball players from different countries, genders, and professional leagues. The dataset includes variables such as nationality, league country, gender, team name, position, years active, Olympian status, national team membership, championships won, award count, Instagram follower count, Instagram post count, profile URLs, and several content classification variables related to posting behavior. Even though the project originally focused on comparing athletic performance with follower counts, the data collection process gradually showed that social media visibility in professional volleyball seems to be shaped more by cultural, institutional, and national systems than by performance alone. Realizing this became one of the biggest intellectual shifts of the project.

Creating the dataset was much more difficult than I originally expected. Unlike many public datasets that already exist online, I had to build this one manually from scratch. I first created a list of professional volleyball players by looking through Olympic rosters, national teams, and professional league rosters. I intentionally chose athletes from many different countries and leagues so the dataset would represent volleyball globally instead of focusing on just one country or one volleyball system. Geographic diversity became especially important because volleyball has very different levels of popularity depending on the country. In some countries, volleyball is a major sport with huge fanbases and strong media attention, while in others it receives far less visibility even if the athletes compete at equally high levels.

After making the player list, I manually verified each athlete’s Instagram account before collecting any statistics. This became one of the most time-consuming and important parts of the project. Many athletes had fan pages, inactive profiles, duplicate accounts, or repost accounts that could easily be mistaken for the real account. Because of this, I had to verify accounts by checking for verification badges, official team links, posting consistency, and references from national federations or professional clubs. I also eventually added profile URLs directly into the dataset so entries could be checked and verified later.

At first, I underestimated how difficult identity verification would become online. I assumed social media data would be easy to collect, but I quickly realized that platforms like Instagram create a lot of ambiguity. This changed the way I thought about data itself. At the beginning of the semester, I mostly thought of data as objective information that could simply be collected and analyzed. However, creating this dataset showed me that data is shaped by human decisions, platform structures, and methodological limitations at every stage. Even something as simple as deciding which Instagram account was “official” required interpretation and judgment. This made me realize that datasets are not completely neutral collections of facts, but instead constructed systems shaped by choices about inclusion, verification, and categorization.

Computation played an important role in the project, although not always in the way I originally expected. At first, I thought most of the work could be automated using scraping tools and Python scripts. I experimented with Python libraries such as BeautifulSoup, Requests, and Instaloader to automate the collection of Instagram statistics. I also tried using third-party Instagram statistics websites as alternative sources when direct scraping failed. These tools were partially successful for retrieving follower counts, post counts, and organizing the dataset structure. Python scripts also helped standardize numerical formatting, clean CSV files, and organize variables into forms that could later be analyzed.

However, scaling the dataset to the required 500–1,000 records became much more difficult than I expected. The biggest issue was not simply collecting numbers, but verifying identity accurately at a larger scale. Automated scraping methods frequently failed because Instagram aggressively blocks scraping attempts, limits requests, and changes page structures regularly. More importantly, automation could not consistently tell the difference between official athlete accounts and fan pages, repost accounts, inactive profiles, or duplicate usernames. This became especially difficult for international players whose names appeared in multiple spellings or languages.

I tried several forms of scaled automation. First, I attempted direct Instagram scraping using BeautifulSoup and Requests. Sometimes these methods worked, but they often returned blocked pages, login redirects, or incomplete information. I then experimented with Instaloader, which worked better for some public accounts but still failed unpredictably because of rate limits and authentication issues. Finally, I explored third-party Instagram statistics websites to automate data retrieval indirectly, but these websites often had outdated or incorrect information.

At smaller scales, these problems could usually be corrected manually. At larger scales, however, verification became the biggest limitation. Even when automated tools successfully retrieved statistics, I still had to manually confirm whether the account actually belonged to the athlete. Without manual verification, the dataset risked including fan pages, impersonation accounts, or incorrect profiles, which would seriously weaken the reliability of the research. Because of this, I eventually decided to prioritize accuracy and verification over simply increasing the size of the dataset as quickly as possible. Looking back, I think a fully scaled version of this project would probably require access to an official API or an already verified sports identity database rather than relying completely on open web scraping.

Another important computational component involved the content classification variables I later added to the dataset, such as athletic content percentage, lifestyle content percentage, sponsorship content percentage, fan interaction percentage, and national identity content percentage. These percentages were created through manual coding of recent Instagram posts. I reviewed athlete posts and categorized them based on the dominant themes shown in the content. Posts focused on games, training, or volleyball activity were classified as athletic content, while posts about travel, fashion, family, or daily life were classified as lifestyle content. Sponsorship posts included advertisements or brand partnerships. Fan interaction content included posts directly engaging with audiences, while national identity content included references to national teams, flags, or international tournaments.

Although this coding process involved subjectivity, it became important because it expanded the project beyond raw popularity numbers. These variables helped frame athletes not only as performers, but also as cultural figures who present themselves differently online depending on branding, nationalism, sponsorship expectations, and audience engagement. This coding process also reinforced another major lesson from the project: cultural data often requires interpretation that cannot be fully automated.

Scale also changed the project conceptually over time. As the dataset became larger, patterns started appearing that were not obvious in smaller samples. Nationality appeared to heavily influence follower counts. Players from Japan, Brazil, Turkey, Italy, and Poland often had dramatically larger audiences than athletes from countries where volleyball has less cultural visibility, even when the athletes competed at similarly elite levels.

These observations shifted the project away from the simple idea that “better performance equals more followers” and toward a broader cultural analysis of visibility itself. Athletes are not only competitors; they are also representatives of nations, media systems, sponsorship structures, and fan cultures. Japanese volleyball players, for example, often had extremely large followings because volleyball has a strong fan culture and media presence in Japan. Brazilian and Turkish athletes also benefited from stronger national volleyball infrastructures and media attention. Meanwhile, equally talented athletes from countries where volleyball receives less visibility often had much smaller online audiences.

The dataset therefore reveals several important aspects of modern sports culture. First, it shows how digital platforms transform athletes into public online brands whose visibility is shaped by much more than athletic performance. Second, it demonstrates how globalization affects sports media by giving athletes from certain countries or leagues much larger international visibility. Third, it shows how online popularity is closely connected to systems such as Olympic participation, national representation, sponsorships, and media exposure.

At the same time, the dataset also hides many important aspects of athlete identity and experience. Social media metrics cannot fully capture emotional connection, fan relationships, cultural meaning, or the qualitative ways audiences interact with athletes. Follower counts provide numerical visibility, but they do not explain how audiences actually interpret or engage with athletes online. The dataset also cannot fully account for algorithmic amplification, sponsorship deals, language barriers, or recommendation systems that may shape visibility. In this sense, the dataset both reveals and conceals culture at the same time.

Ethical and privacy considerations also became important during the project. Even though all information was collected from public profiles, I realized that public availability does not completely remove ethical concerns. Athletes may publicly post aspects of their lives online, but turning that information into structured datasets still raises questions about interpretation, representation, and consent. I also needed to avoid misrepresenting athletes through incorrect account identification or overly simplistic comparisons between popularity and athletic ability.

Another ethical concern involved avoiding reductive interpretations of visibility. One of the most important lessons I learned was that social media popularity is not an objective measure of athletic value. Treating follower counts as neutral indicators of success risks reinforcing existing inequalities in global sports media systems. Athletes from countries with less commercial visibility may receive much less online attention despite competing at the same professional level as globally famous players.

Throughout the semester, I learned that cultural data is fundamentally messy and unstable. Usernames change, accounts disappear, statistics fluctuate daily, and identities become difficult to standardize across countries and platforms. At first, I viewed these inconsistencies as technical problems that needed to be fixed. Over time, however, I realized that these instabilities are actually part of the digital systems being studied. The difficulty of verifying identity online reflects larger issues surrounding visibility, platform governance, and digital representation.

This project also connects to broader peer-reviewed scholarship surrounding sports media, digital identity, and platform culture. Scholars such as David Rowe have written extensively about globalization and sports media, particularly how digital technologies reshape the relationship between athletes, audiences, and national identity. Similarly, Alice Marwick’s work on online self-branding and microcelebrity helps explain how athletes increasingly function as public digital brands whose visibility depends on continuous audience engagement and platform performance. Scholarship surrounding influencer culture and platform capitalism also provides a framework for understanding how athletes operate simultaneously as competitors, media figures, and content creators within attention economies.

Ultimately, the biggest intellectual shift of this project was moving away from viewing social media metrics as straightforward indicators of popularity or athletic performance. Instead, I came to understand visibility itself as something culturally produced through overlapping systems of nationalism, media infrastructure, institutional prestige, branding, globalization, and fan culture. The dataset does not simply measure followers; it captures traces of how modern sports culture functions within digital media environments.

What began as a relatively straightforward social media dataset ultimately became an exploration of how athletes become visible cultural figures online. The project demonstrated both the possibilities and limitations of computational approaches to cultural research. Computation made organization, cleaning, and partial scaling possible, but interpretation, verification, and contextual understanding still remained deeply human processes. Most importantly, the project showed me that data is never simply “found.” It is constructed through methodological decisions, technological limitations, and cultural assumptions that shape what becomes visible and what remains hidden.
