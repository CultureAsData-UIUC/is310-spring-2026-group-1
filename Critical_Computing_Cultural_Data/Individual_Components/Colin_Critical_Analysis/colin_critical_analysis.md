**Response:**

Boatwright’s article is a methodological and theoretical position essay arguing that sport communication research should move beyond small, platform-specific, manually coded studies and adopt Computational Social Science (CSS) to study social media at the scale and complexity it actually exists. The “data” in this article are not a single original dataset collected by the author, but rather the classes of digital trace data that sport scholars can use: large-scale social media texts, replies, comments, hashtags, geotags, user interactions, platform metadata, images, videos, audio, and other naturally occurring behavioral traces from platforms such as Twitter/X, YouTube, Tumblr, Instagram, TikTok, and related online environments. Boatwright’s reasoning is that these data are valuable because they are high-volume, high-velocity, varied, relational, and often fine-grained enough to capture communication as it unfolds, rather than relying only on surveys, interviews, or small samples. In his view, computational methods matter because they let researchers analyze whole corpora or networks instead of just snapshots, trace communication longitudinally, connect messages to behavior, and generate both explanatory and predictive insights. At the same time, he insists that the point is not to replace theory with data; rather, new forms of data should be used to expand, test, and refine theory in sport communication, especially around fandom, engagement, identity, and globalized sport media use.

The computational methods Boatwright surveys are broad, and he explains them in terms of how each one transforms raw social media data into analyzable structure. For text data, he highlights NLP methods such as topic modeling, semantic network analysis, and sentiment analysis: topic modeling uses word distributions across large text corpora to infer recurring themes; semantic network analysis uses word co-occurrence patterns to map how meanings and conversations are structured; and sentiment analysis applies lexicons or trained models to estimate emotional tone in posts, comments, or replies. For relational data, he discusses social network analysis, which converts interactions such as mentions, replies, and ties between users into nodes and edges so scholars can measure influence, centrality, community structure, and homophily. For location-aware data, geospatial analysis uses geotags and inferred location information to examine where fandom and engagement are concentrated and how place shapes sport communication. For behavioral prediction, predictive analytics uses social media actions and metadata—such as posting patterns, mentions, engagement measures, timestamps, or user behavior—to model outcomes like game results, consumer response, recruiting decisions, or ticket sales. He also points to image, video, audio, and part-of-speech analysis for non-text-heavy platforms, arguing that these are increasingly necessary because sport communication now happens through multimodal content, not just written posts. Throughout, his reasoning is that each method requires specific forms of cleaning and restructuring—such as removing stop words for topic models or creating edge tables for network analysis—and that scholars must justify method choice, explain preprocessing, and prioritize “good” data over merely “big” data so results remain interpretable, transparent, and theoretically useful.
